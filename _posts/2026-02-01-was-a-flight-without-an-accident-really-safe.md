Safety has always been one of the most important values in aviation.

When I was learning to fly, and later when I worked as a flight instructor, safety was always at the center of training. From preflight briefings and checklists to repeated procedures and training standards, safety was treated as both the starting point and the final measure of every flight.

But looking back, there is one question I had not seriously considered for a long time.

**If a flight did not result in an accident, was it truly safe?**

As both a pilot and instructor, it is easy to accept the idea that a flight without an accident was a safe flight. In many ways, that assumption feels natural.

However, while studying aviation safety from a broader systems perspective, I began to realize that this assumption may not be wrong, but it may be incomplete.

This post begins with that question and introduces two ways of thinking about aviation safety: **Safety-I** and **Safety-II**.

## The Familiar View of Safety: Safety-I

Most traditional approaches to aviation safety have developed around what is often called **Safety-I**.

The basic idea of Safety-I is relatively straightforward:

> Safety means the absence of accidents, failures, errors, or adverse outcomes.

From this perspective, safety becomes a concern when something goes wrong. An accident occurs, an incident is reported, a procedure is violated, or a system fails. Then the organization investigates what happened and tries to prevent the same event from happening again.

In a Safety-I approach, the key questions are usually:

- Why did the accident occur?
- Which rule or procedure was not followed?
- What part of the system failed?
- How can we prevent the same event from happening again?

This is why many safety systems operate mainly after an event has occurred. After an accident or serious incident, investigators analyze the causes, organizations revise procedures, new checklist items may be added, and regulations may be strengthened.

There is a reason people sometimes say that safety rules are written in blood.

This approach has played a major role in making aviation as safe as it is today. Studying accidents, identifying failures, and strengthening the system to prevent recurrence remain essential parts of aviation safety.

Safety-I is not wrong. It is still one of the foundations of modern aviation safety.

## The Question Safety-I Does Not Fully Answer

However, Safety-I also leaves us with an important question.

Aviation accidents are rare. Most flights end without an accident, without a report, and without any visible failure.

So the question becomes:

**Were all of those flights truly successful simply because nothing bad happened?**

Safety-I focuses mainly on situations where something went wrong. But real flight operations are constantly exposed to variability, uncertainty, workload, weather, time pressure, communication issues, and human limitations.

In other words, everyday flight operations are not perfectly stable or risk-free. Pilots and aviation systems constantly adjust to changing conditions.

Safety-I can explain failure very well.

But by itself, it does not fully explain why everyday operations usually succeed.

## Safety-II: A Different Way to Think About Safety

This is where **Safety-II** provides a different perspective.

Safety-II is a concept developed by aviation safety researcher Erik Hollnagel and introduced systematically in his book *Safety-I and Safety-II: The Past and Future of Safety Management*.

In Safety-II, the definition of safety changes.

> Safety is the ability to succeed under varying conditions.

Instead of focusing only on why things went wrong, Safety-II asks why things usually go right.

From this perspective, the more important questions become:

- Why did this flight end safely?
- How did the system absorb or manage human error?
- What judgments helped maintain safety in a changing situation?
- How did the pilot adapt to conditions that were not perfectly ideal?

Hollnagel explains this through the concept of **resilience**.

In aviation, resilience can be understood as the ability of people, organizations, and systems to adapt to changing conditions while continuing to function safely.

This does not mean ignoring rules or replacing procedures with individual judgment. Rather, Safety-II recognizes that real-world operations often require people to adjust, interpret, and adapt within the boundaries of the system.

## Does “No Accident” Always Mean “Safe”?

A landing can help illustrate this idea.

If an aircraft lands without an accident, was that landing automatically safe?

Imagine an approach where the aircraft was unstable, airspeed and descent rate continued to fluctuate, and the landing was completed only after several last-minute corrections. The aircraft touched down safely, and no damage occurred.

From a narrow Safety-I perspective, this flight might be recorded as a normal operation. If the pilot did not file a report and no accident occurred, the event may never be discussed.

But from a Safety-II perspective, the process matters.

The flight may have ended safely, but the operation may still have contained a high level of risk. The important question is not only whether the landing was completed, but how that outcome was achieved.

Safety-II would ask:

- Why did the flight end safely despite the unstable conditions?
- Would the same situation be safe if it happened again tomorrow?
- Are we relying too heavily on an individual pilot’s last-minute judgment?
- What did the pilot do to prevent the situation from becoming worse?
- What can the system learn from this successful but high-risk outcome?

These questions are not meant to blame the pilot.

In fact, Safety-II often does the opposite. It tries to understand how human judgment, adaptation, and experience help the system succeed when conditions are not ideal.

This is an important distinction.

Safety-II is not a rejection of Safety-I. It does not argue that rules, checklists, procedures, or accident investigation are unnecessary.

Instead, Safety-II builds on Safety-I by asking us to also study normal work, successful outcomes, and the everyday adjustments that keep aviation safe.

## Closing Thoughts

The absence of an accident does not always mean that a flight was fully safe.

Aviation safety is increasingly moving beyond judging safety only by the final outcome. It is also about understanding the process that produced that outcome.

If Safety-I helped build the foundation of modern aviation safety by asking why accidents happen, Safety-II adds another important question:

**Why do most flights go well?**

After a flight, it is easy to say, “Today's flight was safe.”

But perhaps the more important question is:

**How did we get here safely?**

I believe that asking this question can help pilots, instructors, researchers, and aviation organizations build a deeper and more practical understanding of safety.
