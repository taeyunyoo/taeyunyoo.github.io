*Adapted from my original Korean-language aviation blog post.*

In August, I will become a student again.

After working as a flight instructor for nearly two years and building close to 1,000 flight hours, I reached an important turning point in my aviation journey. For a long time, I had imagined my path in aviation in a fairly traditional way: flight training, instructing, building hours, and eventually moving toward airline operations.

When I first began working as a flight instructor, I also saw the role as part of that pathway. It was a necessary step, a professional stage, and a way to continue developing as a pilot.

But over time, something changed.

I began to find deeper meaning not only in flying, but in teaching.

## Finding Meaning in Flight Instruction

As a flight instructor, some of the most meaningful moments came from helping students understand something that had once seemed confusing or overwhelming.

Revising lesson plans, explaining concepts in different ways, reviewing mistakes together, and watching students grow through repeated practice became more rewarding than I had expected. Flight instruction gradually became more than just a job or a stepping stone.

It became a place where I could see how aviation knowledge, decision-making, communication, and safety culture are shaped in real time.

I also learned that teaching is not a one-way process. Students grow through their instructors, but instructors also grow through their students. Each student brought different questions, difficulties, strengths, and perspectives. Those interactions pushed me to study more deeply, explain more clearly, and think more carefully about what it means to train a pilot safely.

That experience changed how I saw my own future in aviation.

## Wanting to Go Deeper

During my time as an instructor, I often asked questions to more experienced pilots and instructors. Some of the most discouraging answers I heard were along the lines of, “You don't need to know that,” or “That won't be on the checkride.”

I understood the practical intent behind those comments. Flight training is structured around standards, checkrides, procedures, and required knowledge. But I also felt that aviation education should not stop at minimum requirements.

That reaction may have shaped me more than I realized.

I became increasingly interested in the questions behind the procedures:

Why do we teach this concept this way?  
How do pilots actually make decisions under pressure?  
How do training systems shape safety behavior?  
How can aviation safety be understood not only through rules, but also through data, systems, and human performance?

The more I reflected on these questions, the clearer my direction became.

I wanted to study aviation safety and education more deeply.

## Returning to Graduate School

By the time I had nearly reached the flight time requirements that many instructors use as a transition point, I found myself thinking less about the next cockpit seat and more about the broader field of aviation safety.

I was still deeply connected to flying, but my professional interests had begun to shift toward safety research and education.

That is why I decided to return to Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University for graduate study in Aviation, with a specialization in Aviation Safety Systems.

My goal is not to step away from aviation operations, but to understand them from a broader perspective. I want to connect what I learned in the cockpit and in the classroom with academic tools that can help explain risk, improve training, and support safer decision-making.

In the future, I hope to continue this path through doctoral study and contribute as a researcher, educator, and mentor in aviation safety.

## Continuing the Blog

Through this blog, I have shared many experiences from the perspective of a pilot and flight instructor.

Going forward, I hope to expand that perspective.

I plan to write more about aviation safety, flight training, human factors, systems thinking, safety management, and research methods in a way that remains practical and accessible. My goal is to connect the lessons learned from flight operations with the ideas I encounter through graduate study and aviation safety research.

In that sense, this blog will continue to be a bridge between practice and study.

What I learned in the airplane can inform how I think about research.  
What I learn through research can reshape how I understand flight training and safety.

## Closing Thoughts

This decision was not easy.

Changing direction always involves uncertainty. It requires time, commitment, and a willingness to step into a new phase without knowing exactly where it will lead.

But I believe this path reflects the kind of work I want to pursue.

The altitude and airspeed may be different from the path I once imagined, but the foundation remains the same: aviation, learning, and safety.

This time, I hope to fly farther through knowledge, research, and education.
