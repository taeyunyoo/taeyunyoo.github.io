A stall is one of the most important aerodynamic concepts every pilot must understand.

It is also one of the topics that can feel intimidating to new student pilots. The word “stall” sounds dramatic, and in aviation, it is closely connected to safety, aircraft control, and accident prevention.

In this post, I want to explain stalls from two perspectives: **aerodynamics** and **aviation safety**.

Before discussing stalls, it helps to understand how airplanes generate lift. In a previous post, [How Do Airplanes Fly?](/posts/2024/02/how-do-airplanes-fly/), I explained the basic forces of flight and how wings create lift through airflow, pressure differences, and downwash.

This post builds on that idea.

## What Is a Stall?

In aviation, a stall occurs when the smooth airflow over a wing begins to separate significantly, causing the wing to lose a substantial amount of lift.

A stall does not mean the engine has stopped.

This is one of the first misunderstandings many people have. In everyday language, “stall” often refers to an engine quitting. But in aerodynamics, a stall refers to the wing, not the engine.

A wing produces lift when air flows around it in a controlled and efficient way. The airfoil shape, angle of attack, and airflow around the wing all work together to generate lift.

But if the wing reaches too high of an angle of attack, the airflow can no longer stay smoothly attached to the upper surface of the wing. The airflow separates, lift decreases, and drag increases.

That condition is what pilots call a stall.

## The Key to Stall: Critical Angle of Attack

Now that we have defined what a stall is, the next question is: **what actually happens to the airflow over the wing?**

The answer is more direct than many students expect.

A wing can increase lift as angle of attack increases, but only up to a certain point. Once the wing exceeds that point, the airflow can no longer remain smoothly attached to the upper surface of the wing. The airflow begins to separate, lift decreases, and drag increases rapidly.

That point is called the **critical angle of attack**.

The figure above shows this process clearly.

At a lower angle of attack, the airflow remains mostly smooth over the wing. As the angle of attack increases, airflow separation begins near the trailing edge. The wing may still be producing lift, but it is becoming less efficient and more turbulent.

Once the angle of attack exceeds the critical value, the separation becomes much more severe. At that point, the wing can no longer maintain smooth airflow over the upper surface, and lift drops sharply.

This is the key point:

> A stall occurs when the wing exceeds its critical angle of attack.

This means that an aircraft does not stall simply because it is “too slow.” Low airspeed can make a stall more likely because the pilot may need a higher angle of attack to maintain lift. However, the direct aerodynamic cause of a stall is exceeding the critical angle of attack.

An aircraft can stall at a low airspeed.  
It can also stall at a higher airspeed if the load factor or maneuvering condition causes the wing to exceed its critical AOA.

That is why pilots are taught that an aircraft can stall at **any airspeed, in any attitude, if the critical angle of attack is exceeded**.

The relationship becomes even clearer when we look at the lift curve.

As angle of attack increases, the coefficient of lift also increases. In other words, the wing is producing more lift as the pilot raises the nose or increases the wing’s angle relative to the airflow.

But this trend does not continue forever.

At the critical angle of attack, the wing reaches its maximum lift coefficient. Beyond that point, the airflow separation becomes too great, and the coefficient of lift drops. This sudden reduction in lift is what makes a stall so dangerous, especially close to the ground.

So the important lesson is not simply “do not fly too slowly.”

The better lesson is:

> Manage the wing’s angle of attack and recognize the conditions that can bring the aircraft close to its critical AOA.

## What Happens During a Stall?

As angle of attack increases, lift generally increases at first.

However, after the wing reaches its critical angle of attack, the airflow separation becomes too great. Lift drops, drag increases, and the aircraft may begin to descend rapidly.

Depending on the aircraft and flight condition, the pilot may notice several warning signs before or during a stall:

- decreasing airspeed,
- a high pitch attitude,
- reduced control effectiveness,
- aerodynamic buffet,
- stall warning horn or light,
- increasing sink rate,
- or a tendency for one wing to drop.

These cues are why stall recognition is just as important as stall recovery. The earlier a pilot recognizes the developing stall, the more time and altitude they have to correct it.

## Stall Recovery: Reduce the Angle of Attack

Because a stall is caused by exceeding the critical angle of attack, the first step in stall recovery is simple in concept:

> Reduce the angle of attack.

In practical terms, this usually means lowering the nose enough to allow the wing to regain smooth airflow.

After reducing AOA, the pilot can add power as appropriate, manage pitch, level the wings, minimize altitude loss, and return to a safe flight attitude. The exact procedure depends on the aircraft type, training standard, and operating handbook, but the aerodynamic principle remains the same.

The wing must be unstalled first.

This is why simply pulling back harder on the yoke or trying to “force” the airplane to climb can make the situation worse. If the wing is already stalled, increasing back pressure can increase the angle of attack even more.

Stall recovery requires the pilot to do something that may feel counterintuitive at low altitude:

Lower the nose, reduce the angle of attack, and allow the wing to fly again.

## Why Pilots Practice Stalls

Student pilots practice stalls because stall awareness is directly connected to safety.

In private pilot training, students typically learn power-off stalls and power-on stalls. These help students understand stalls in situations that resemble approach/landing configurations and takeoff/departure configurations.

As training progresses, pilots may also learn about accelerated stalls, cross-controlled stalls, secondary stalls, and other scenarios that demonstrate how stalls can occur in different flight conditions.

These maneuvers are not taught because instructors want students to be comfortable with danger.

They are taught because pilots need to recognize the warning signs early, understand what the aircraft is telling them, and recover correctly before the situation becomes unrecoverable.

## Why Stalls Matter in Aviation Safety

From an aviation safety perspective, stalls are important because they can develop quickly and leave very little time for correction, especially close to the ground.

During takeoff, departure, approach, and landing, the aircraft is often low, slow, and close to terrain. A stall in these phases of flight can become dangerous within seconds.

AOPA statistics from 2000 to 2014 show that stall-related events accounted for nearly one-quarter of fatal accidents.

This is why loss of control and stall-related accidents remain a major concern in general aviation and commercial aviation safety.

Several well-known accidents have involved stall or loss-of-control dynamics, including:

- Colgan Air Flight 3407,
- Air France Flight 447,
- Comair Flight 3272,
- and AirAsia Flight 8501.

Each accident had its own complex chain of contributing factors. A stall should never be understood as a single simple cause. Human factors, automation, training, weather, procedures, workload, and aircraft handling may all play a role.

Still, these accidents remind us that stall awareness is not only a student pilot topic. It remains important across all levels of aviation.

Even highly experienced pilots can be vulnerable when the aircraft is close to the ground, workload is high, or the situation develops faster than expected.

## The Dangerous Simplicity of Stalls

In one sense, the solution sounds simple:

Do not exceed the critical angle of attack.

But in real flight, maintaining that awareness is not always simple.

Pilots manage workload, weather, traffic, communication, checklists, automation, passengers, and time pressure. In that environment, airspeed, pitch attitude, bank angle, and aircraft energy can change quickly.

A stall may begin with small warning signs:

- slightly slow airspeed,
- excessive pitch,
- distraction on final approach,
- overcorrection in a turn,
- failure to manage energy,
- or delayed recognition of a developing situation.

That is why stall training is not only about memorizing a recovery procedure.

It is about building awareness.

A good pilot does not only know how to recover from a stall. A good pilot recognizes the conditions that make a stall more likely and avoids getting there in the first place.

## Closing Thoughts

A stall occurs when a wing exceeds its critical angle of attack and the airflow can no longer remain properly attached.

That explanation may sound simple, but its safety implications are serious.

Stall awareness connects aerodynamics, flight training, human factors, and accident prevention. It teaches pilots to understand what the wing is doing, not just what the airspeed indicator says.

For student pilots, stalls can feel intimidating at first. But with proper instruction, practice, and understanding, stall training becomes one of the most valuable parts of flight education.

The goal is not simply to recover from a stall.

The goal is to recognize, prevent, and manage the conditions that could lead to one.
