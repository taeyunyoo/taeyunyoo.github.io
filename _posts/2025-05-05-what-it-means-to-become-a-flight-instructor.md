*Adapted from my original Korean-language aviation blog post.*

Recently, I completed CFII training with two Korean students I had worked with for a long time.

They were not just students. They were also colleagues I had known since college. Over time, our relationship changed from colleagues, to instructor and students, and eventually to fellow flight instructors standing in the same profession.

There were challenges along the way. There were disagreements, setbacks, and moments when the training process was not easy for any of us.

But both of them completed their CFI and CFII training, and today they are no longer simply my students.

They are flight instructors.

In this post, I want to reflect on what it really means to become a flight instructor, and what I hoped they would carry with them beyond the checkride.

## Instructor and Student as a Team

One of the most important principles I try to hold as an instructor is simple:

**The instructor and student are a team.**

Flight training cannot work well without trust.

If a student no longer feels that the instructor is on their side, the instructor’s words become harder to accept. Feedback may feel like criticism rather than guidance. Standards may feel like pressure rather than protection.

That is why I try to build trust first with every student.

This was especially important with these two students. Because we had known each other as friends before becoming instructor and students, the transition was not always simple. Expectations were different. Communication was not always perfect. At times, we disagreed strongly.

But through those moments, the relationship gradually became one built on trust.

I believe that trust is one of the reasons we were able to complete both the CFI and CFII courses together.

## The Difficulty of CFI Training

CFI training is difficult because it requires more than flying skill.

A pilot may know how to perform a maneuver, but teaching that maneuver to another person is a different task. It requires structure, communication, judgment, patience, and the ability to explain not only what to do, but why it matters.

That is what makes the CFI initial checkride so demanding.

The gap between **flying well** and **teaching well** is larger than many pilots expect.

During the CFI process, one of the students experienced a checkride failure during a flight maneuver. It was disappointing, especially because the maneuver involved a skill that pilots begin practicing early in training.

But it was also a clear learning moment.

A checkride failure is painful. No student wants to fail, and no instructor wants to see a student fail.

But the more important question is what happens after the failure.

What did the student learn?  
How did the instructor respond?  
How will that experience shape the way the future instructor teaches their own students?

If a failure becomes a source of deeper understanding, humility, and better instruction, then it can still become part of professional growth.

I say this as an instructor who has experienced checkride failures firsthand.

A failed checkride does not define a pilot.  
But the response to that failure can shape the kind of pilot, and the kind of instructor, that person becomes.

## Growth Through the CFII Process

When we moved into CFII training, I began to see both students develop their own instructional styles.

They were no longer only trying to meet a standard. They were beginning to think like instructors.

They prepared lessons differently. They explained concepts differently. They responded to feedback differently. The content of the course was similar, but the way each student approached teaching became more personal.

One of the most valuable parts of instructor training, in my view, is giving future instructors a chance to teach real students.

Role-playing with an instructor has value, but it has limits. Teaching an actual student creates a different kind of pressure. It forces the instructor applicant to organize the lesson clearly, respond to confusion, adjust explanations, manage time, and notice whether the student is actually learning.

For that reason, I often try to create opportunities for instructor applicants to observe or teach real lessons to student pilots when appropriate.

With these two students, however, I approached the process differently for each of them. Based on their preparation styles and learning needs, one benefited from more direct teaching practice with actual students, while the other benefited more from refining lesson structure and checkride preparation without taking on the same amount of live teaching.

There was no single method that worked best for both.

That, too, is part of instruction.

Good teaching is not only about delivering the same material the same way every time. It is about understanding what each student needs in order to grow.

By the time they reached their CFII checkrides, both students had grown in ways that went beyond technical knowledge.

They had begun to understand what it means to stand in front of another student as an instructor.

And eventually, both completed their CFII checkrides successfully.

I was happy not only because they passed, but because I felt they had become ready to teach.

## The Certificate Is Only the Beginning

Today, both of them are working as flight instructors.

One joined the flight school where I worked, and the other found an opportunity at another Part 141 flight school.

In a time when instructor positions can be highly competitive, that achievement is meaningful. But employment is not the end of the story.

When we first began the CFI process, I told them that instructor training is one of the last protected stages before they enter the field as teachers responsible for someone else’s safety and development.

A CFI certificate is not the end.

It is the beginning.

A certificate does not automatically make someone a better pilot. It does not automatically add knowledge, judgment, humility, or professionalism.

Those have to be built on top of the certificate.

To become a real instructor, a pilot has to add responsibility, care, discipline, and a commitment to continuous learning.

## The Last Dinner

After both students passed their CFII checkrides, the three of us had dinner together.

That night, I told them something I also try to remind myself:

> Never become arrogant just because you are now an instructor.  
> Fly with the caution of someone who is still learning.  
> Stay humble, stay careful, and keep learning.

Those words were not only for them.

They were also for me.

During my time as a flight instructor, I experienced many difficult moments. I made mistakes. I saw students struggle. I learned from situations that could have gone differently if I had not been careful.

Every instructor has to remember that aviation does not forgive carelessness easily.

We are all capable of making mistakes. But in flight, some mistakes can become serious very quickly. That is why skill matters. Judgment matters. Humility matters.

And that is why I believe instructors should always remember what it felt like to be a student pilot for the first time.

## Closing Thoughts

These two pilots are no longer my students.

They are now instructors who will guide someone else through their first flights, first mistakes, first landings, and first moments of confidence in the cockpit.

I hope they remember the importance of trust.

I hope they remember that instruction is not only about transferring knowledge, but also about shaping judgment, attitude, and safety culture.

And I hope they remember that becoming a flight instructor is not simply about earning a certificate.

It is about accepting responsibility for another person’s learning, safety, and growth.

That was what I truly wanted to teach.

And to both of them:  
Congratulations to both of you.  
You worked incredibly hard—and, honestly, so did I.
