*Adapted from my original Korean-language aviation blog post.*

I debated for a while whether I should write about this topic, because it felt heavier than what I usually write.

Still, I decided to share it in the hope that it may be helpful to pilots, flight instructors, student pilots, and those who are preparing to begin flight training.

Please read this as a personal column rather than a formal research article.

## I Did Not Take Safety Seriously

When I first started flight training at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University after completing my military service in 2019, I did not think that deeply about safety.

Before starting flight training, I had looked up some aviation accident statistics. What I found was actually reassuring. Compared to driving, flying seemed statistically much safer, and that gave me a sense of comfort.

I had heard that there had been a serious accident at Embry-Riddle about a year before I started flying, but at the time, it did not feel very personal to me.

The Private Pilot course was much harder than I expected. Embry-Riddle’s complex flight procedures and safety rules felt like things I simply had to study.

They felt less like “things I do for safe flying” and more like “things I need to memorize and perform correctly.”

Honestly, they felt inconvenient.

That perspective began to change before my Private Pilot checkride, when I flew with another instructor.

During the preflight inspection, I noticed that the instructor firmly grabbed one of the wings and shook it with force. I asked why, because I did not remember seeing that action in the Preflight Checklist.

The instructor brought up a previous [accident](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/era18fa120.aspx) involving a Piper PA-28 that had experienced an in-flight wing separation during a checkride.

After the flight, I went home and read more about the accident. It was a tragic accident in April 2018, where an Embry-Riddle PA-28 broke up in flight during a commercial checkride, killing both the student and the examiner.

What surprised me was that this was not simply a case of student pilot error. The examiner must have had thousands of hours of flight experience, yet the accident was related to an aircraft failure.

After that accident, Embry-Riddle replaced its PA-28 fleet with Cessna 172 aircraft, which made me feel a little more comfortable.

But since then, giving the wing a firm shake during preflight became part of my personal routine.

## All Those Checklists

Embry-Riddle has many checklists.

From before engine start to after shutdown, almost every procedure is done with a checklist. If I missed even one item, I would often get corrected by my instructor.

Many checklists could simply be read and followed. But the checklists labeled as “Flow” items and the Emergency Checklist had to be memorized, which was not easy.

Time passed, and one day I was doing a commercial solo flight.

I was returning to Daytona Beach and was on short final for landing when I heard a strange rattling sound. I could not immediately tell what it was, but something felt off.

I was already on short final, so I quickly scanned the airplane.

Trim looked fine.

Flaps were full.

Power was fine.

Then I noticed it.

The mixture was not full rich.

“Mixture full rich” was included in both the Arrival Checklist and the Before Landing Final Items. Yet somehow, I had missed it twice and was about to land with the mixture still leaned.

I immediately pushed the mixture to full rich and landed safely.

After checking in, I texted my instructor that I had returned safely. Of course, I did not mention what had happened during landing.

But I knew I had to be more careful.

That was the first time I truly felt that checklists exist for a reason.

## Where Would I Land If the Engine Quit Here?

In the Commercial Pilot course, there is a solo long cross-country flight that requires about six hours of flight time.

I originally wanted to fly to Key West, so I planned a route there. But the weather did not cooperate, and I had to cancel several times.

After a few weeks, I was scheduled again. The weather in Key West was still not good, but the weather to the north looked better. So I stayed up through the night and changed my plan to fly a different route.

The route was KDAB–KDHN–KTLH–KDAB.

Before the flight, I thought I might be worried about getting tired or sleepy. But once I was in the airplane, what I felt was not boredom.

It was tension.

There was only one thought that kept coming back to me:

“What if the engine quits right now?”

I had practiced engine failure procedures many times during Private and Commercial training. But this flight felt different. It was a long flight, and this time there was no instructor sitting next to me.

For most of the flight, I kept reviewing the Emergency Checklist in my head and looking outside for places where I could land if I had to.

Fortunately, nothing happened.

But after that flight, looking for possible emergency landing fields became a habit.

I mentally simulated engine failures again and again, sometimes to the point where I caught myself wondering whether I could actually handle it if the engine really quit.

## CFI Training and Safety

I think I began to care about safety more seriously during my CFI training.

I completed my CFI training at Epic Flight Academy. The course itself was demanding and exhausting.

Looking back, I do not know if any flight training course ever felt easy. But because I was also working through commercial multi-engine training at Embry-Riddle at the same time, the CFI course was probably one of the most mentally and physically difficult periods of my training.

Before I started flying at Epic, I spent about a month in ground instruction.

I learned a great deal from that instructor. He later moved on to the airlines, but if I had to choose one of my mentors in aviation, he would be one of the first people I would think of.

What I remember most was not only what he taught, but also his mindset.

As a student pilot, there was always someone protecting me.

As a flight instructor, that would no longer be the case.

Not only would I have to protect myself, but I would also be responsible for protecting my students.

That responsibility felt heavy.

On the final day of CFI training, he talked to us about the mindset of a flight instructor.

He began by saying something along the lines of:

“Some flight schools just want to keep the propellers running.”

His point was that as instructors and pilots in command, we must be willing to make the right go/no-go decision. If the weather, the aircraft condition, or even a small concern makes the flight feel unsafe, we should be willing to cancel.

He also said that if a flight school ever pressures an instructor to fly in a situation where the instructor believes the flight should be canceled, then the problem is with the flight school.

That message stayed with me.

It also made me realize that I had been somewhat protected by the environment I trained in. At Embry-Riddle, if weather or an aircraft issue made a flight unsafe, canceling the flight was accepted. I had not fully appreciated how important that kind of safety culture was.

Later, I remember seeing a [video](https://youtu.be/UX_oO6wGD94?si=g22SlAmfR4wd8zZ0) about an airline captain who refused to fly because of an aircraft concern, even after being told that maintenance considered the aircraft acceptable.

It reminded me of what I learned during CFI training.

The pressure that captain faced was probably far greater than anything I had experienced as an instructor. Yet he still made a no-go decision. Seeing that reminded me again that safety decisions are not always easy, but they matter.

## Students and Unexpected Errors

During CFI training, I heard a phrase that many instructors probably know:

“Students are going to try to kill you in every single way you can think of.”

Once I started working as a flight instructor, I understood what that meant.

Hard landings were just the beginning.

A student might get distracted during taxi and nearly cross a runway without clearance.

A student might shut down the engine during run-up.

A student might line up for the wrong runway.

Students can make mistakes in ways I expected, and sometimes in ways I never imagined.

After working for more than a year as an instructor and flying with many students, I am sure there are still many types of mistakes I have not yet seen.

The ironic part is that whenever I started to feel frustrated, I would remember my own student pilot days.

I once confused Runway 7R and 7L during a night flight and nearly landed on the wrong runway.

I had hard landings.

I had exceeded flap speed limitations.

I had missed checklist items.

So instead of simply getting upset, I began trying to debrief more carefully after landing. I would go back through what happened, explain why it was risky, and help the student understand how to recognize it earlier next time.

As I gained experience, I also became more patient.

If a mistake was not immediately dangerous, I sometimes allowed the student to experience it and learn from it, rather than correcting everything too early.

My goal became less about preventing every small mistake in the moment and more about helping students remember the lesson five or ten years later.

I wanted them to care about safety not because an instructor told them to, but because they understood why it mattered.

## Accidents Around Me

Maybe I became more aware because I was working as an instructor.

Maybe it was because many of the people around me also graduated and became instructors.

Or maybe it was simply the timing.

But in recent years, I began noticing more accidents and incidents around the flight training community.

Aviation accidents are more common than many people think.

In the flight training community, small and large events happen regularly. Even at schools that care deeply about safety, incidents still occur. They may not always become major news, but they happen.

I remember hearing about an [accident](https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/310128) involving an Embry-Riddle DA42 in 2023, where the airplane had a hard landing and departed the runway.

I also remember hearing about [accidents](https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/388375) involving other flight schools in the Daytona Beach area.

Some accidents resulted in injuries.

Others were fatal.

One [accident](https://www.fox5atlanta.com/news/single-engine-plane-crashes-paulding-county-during-training-flight) was especially difficult to process because it involved an instructor who had graduated from Embry-Riddle around the same time as me. I did not know the full details of the accident, but the name and face were familiar enough that it felt much closer than other accidents I had read about.

Even during normal work, small safety events continue to happen.

I have seen an Embry-Riddle airplane return after takeoff due to a tailstrike.

I have experienced a bird strike with a student during a night flight.

I have seen an airplane ahead of me reject a takeoff after a nose tire problem.

These events may not all become accidents, but they are reminders.

Aviation safety is not something that only matters after something goes wrong.

It is present in ordinary flights, ordinary decisions, and ordinary habits.

## Why I Care About Safety

The reason I care about safety is simple.

I want to come home safely.

And I want the people around me, especially the students I teach, to learn in a way that helps them avoid becoming part of an accident story someday.

As I continued working as a flight instructor, I began developing student training resources to support more structured preparation.

I revised pre-solo written exam materials.

I created exam-preparation guides.

I continued updating my lesson plans, sometimes multiple times in the same week.

The more I learned, the more I realized how much more there was to understand.

That is how I became a pilot who cares about safety.

Not because I was always that way.

But because my experiences as a pilot, student, and instructor slowly changed the way I think.

Safety is not just a rule.

It is not just a checklist.

It is not just something we talk about after an accident.

Safety is a habit, a mindset, and a responsibility.

And that is why I care about aviation safety.
