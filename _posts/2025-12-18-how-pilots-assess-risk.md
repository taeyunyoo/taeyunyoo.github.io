When flying, pilots often hear or think phrases like:

“This should be fine.”

“It is a flight I have done many times before, so it should not be a problem.”

However, many aviation accidents do not occur because of completely new hazards.

They often happen when existing hazards are treated as ordinary, familiar, or not serious enough to change the plan.

For that reason, the aviation industry does not leave safety entirely to instinct or experience.

Instead, safety is addressed through a structured concept known as **risk management**.

Risk management is important enough that the FAA has a separate publication called the [**Risk Management Handbook**](https://www.faa.gov/regulationspolicies/handbooksmanuals/risk-management-handbook-faa-h-8083-2a), which is widely used in pilot training.

It is especially emphasized during flight instructor training, where future instructors must learn how to guide students who may have limited flight experience.

In this post, I will introduce the basic meanings of **hazard**, **risk**, and **safety** in aviation, and then discuss several risk assessment tools that pilots and safety professionals use.

## Hazard, Risk, and Safety

Many student pilots confuse **hazard** and **risk**, so it is helpful to begin with how these terms are used in aviation.

A **hazard** is a potential source of danger that could contribute to an accident.

The FAA Risk Management Handbook defines a hazard as:

> “A condition that could foreseeably cause or contribute to an aircraft accident as defined in 49 CFR part 830, section 830.2.”

Examples of hazards may include:

- Poor weather
- A fatigued pilot
- An unfamiliar airport
- An aircraft with unclear maintenance history

These factors do not automatically cause an accident by themselves.

However, they can become the starting point of an accident sequence.

**Risk**, on the other hand, refers to the likelihood and severity of the possible outcome created by a hazard.

The FAA Risk Management Handbook describes risk as:

> “The composite of predicted severity and likelihood of the potential effect of a hazard.”

To put it more simply, a thunderstorm itself is a hazard.

But if a pilot chooses to fly near or into that weather, the possible outcomes may include loss of control, system failure, or controlled flight into terrain.

Those outcomes can be evaluated based on how likely they are and how severe the consequences would be.

That is where risk comes in.

Finally, **safety** does not mean a state where all hazards and risks have completely disappeared.

In aviation, safety is better understood as a condition in which hazards and risks are identified, understood, and managed to an acceptable level.

In other words, safety is not the absence of risk.

It is the active management of risk.

## Why Risk Management Matters to Pilots

One thing most pilots would agree on is that no two flights are exactly the same.

Even if the departure airport and destination are the same, many variables can change from one flight to another:

- Weather
- Pilot experience
- Aircraft type
- Airport environment
- Passengers
- Pilot physical and mental condition

Because of these variables, the level of risk changes every time.

For example, imagine two pilots flying on the same day with poor visibility.

One pilot may have just earned a Private Pilot Certificate.

The other may be an airline pilot with twenty years of experience.

The weather hazard may be the same, but the level of risk faced by each pilot can be very different.

That is why pilots need the ability to identify, assess, and mitigate risk in a structured way.

Risk assessment tools are designed to support that process.

## FRAT: A Common Tool for Pilots

</p>

One of the most commonly used tools in flight operations is the **Flight Risk Assessment Tool**, or **FRAT**.

A FRAT helps pilots evaluate the overall risk of a flight by assigning scores to different risk factors before departure.

Many FRATs are organized around the **PAVE** model:

**P — Pilot**  
Fatigue, recent flight experience, certificate level, health, and readiness.

**A — Aircraft**  
Aircraft familiarity, maintenance condition, equipment, and performance.

**V — enVironment**  
Weather, airport environment, night operations, terrain, airspace complexity, and other external operating conditions.

**E — External Pressures**  
Schedule pressure, passenger expectations, get-there-itis, or the feeling that the flight “must” happen today.

Each item receives a score, and the total score gives the pilot a clearer picture of the overall risk level.

If the score exceeds a certain threshold, the pilot may decide to delay the flight, change the plan, add another qualified pilot, or take other risk mitigation actions.

In short, FRAT gives pilots a structured opportunity to pause, evaluate the flight objectively, and adjust the plan before risk becomes harder to manage.

Another useful tool is the **Risk Assessment Matrix**.

Unlike a FRAT, which usually scores multiple factors across a flight, a risk assessment matrix evaluates a specific risk by comparing two dimensions:

- **Likelihood**: How likely is the event to occur?
- **Severity**: How serious would the consequences be?

Let’s return to the example of poor visibility.

If a pilot loses control of the aircraft in instrument meteorological conditions, the severity could be catastrophic or critical.

However, the likelihood of that outcome may be very different depending on the pilot.

A newly certificated private pilot and an experienced airline pilot may face the same weather, but their likelihood of losing control in that environment may be very different.

As a result, the same general hazard may fall into a high-risk category for one pilot and a lower-risk category for another.

This is why the risk assessment matrix is useful.

It helps pilots think beyond the simple question:

“Is this dangerous?”

Instead, it encourages a more structured question:

“How likely is this to happen, and how serious would the outcome be if it does?”

## Other Safety Risk Management Tools

So far, we have discussed tools that are commonly introduced to pilots during training.

However, the broader field of aviation safety uses many additional tools to evaluate risk across aircraft, operations, organizations, and systems.

Pilots do not necessarily need to use all of these tools in daily operations.

Still, understanding that they exist helps show that aviation safety is not only about individual flying skill.

It is also about system-level risk management.

## Bowtie Analysis

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/bowtie.png"
       alt="Bowtie risk analysis model"
       style="width: 600px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/solutions/enablon/bowtie/expert-insights/barrier-based-risk-management-knowledge-base/the-bowtie-method" target="_blank">Wolters Kluwer</a>.</em>
</p>

A **Bowtie** model visualizes the relationship between threats, a central hazardous event, and possible consequences.

It also shows preventive and recovery barriers on both sides of the event.

The diagram is called a bowtie because of its shape.

It is useful for understanding how threats can lead to an undesired event, and how different barriers can prevent or reduce the consequences.

## ARMS-ERC

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/ARMS-ERC.png"
       alt="ARMS Event Risk Classification matrix"
       style="width: 650px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/easy-access-rules/online-publications/easy-access-rules-occurrence-reporting?page=10" target="_blank">EASA</a>.</em>
</p>

**ARMS** stands for Aviation Risk Management Solutions.

The Event Risk Classification, or **ERC**, is a tool used to classify the risk level of aviation events.

It considers both the potential severity of an event and the likelihood that it could have escalated into a more serious outcome.

In this way, ERC helps safety professionals evaluate not only what happened, but also what could have happened.

## STAMP-CAST

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/STAMP-CAST.png"

  <br>

**STAMP** stands for System-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes.

**CAST**, or Causal Analysis based on STAMP, is an accident analysis method built on the STAMP framework. Rather than focusing only on individual error or component failure, CAST examines accident causes through failures in control and feedback within complex systems.

## FRAM

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/fram.png"
       alt="Functional Resonance Analysis Method model"
       style="width: 500px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/figure/nstantiation-of-FRAM-model-for-solution-issuing-evaluation-and-approval_fig5_330831936" target="_blank">José J. Cañas</a>.</em>
</p>

**FRAM**, or Functional Resonance Analysis Method, examines how normal variability in everyday work can combine in unexpected ways.

Instead of assuming that accidents only happen because someone violated a fixed procedure, FRAM focuses on how real-world operations vary and adapt.

In aviation, this can be useful because flight operations are rarely perfectly static.

Pilots, controllers, maintenance personnel, and organizations constantly adapt to changing conditions.

Sometimes those adaptations work well.

Other times, several small variations can combine and produce an unexpected outcome.

## Closing Thoughts

Risk management is inseparable from flying.

At times, it can make flight planning feel more complex and demanding.

But if we want to reduce risk, we first need to recognize and evaluate it.

That is why many flight schools require FRATs or similar risk assessment forms as part of the preflight process.

Ultimately, true flying skill is not only shown by how smoothly a pilot controls the aircraft.

It is also shown by how early the pilot recognizes risk and how effectively the pilot manages it.
