*Adapted from my original Korean-language aviation blog post.*

A few weeks ago, after finishing a ground lesson, I came across a post online explaining how airplanes fly.

At first, I thought it was a clear and simple explanation. But as I kept reading, I noticed a description that surprised me. It reminded me that even in aviation, some of the explanations we hear early in training can be incomplete or misleading.

So in this post, I want to revisit a simple but important question:

**How does an airplane fly?**

Whether it is a small training aircraft weighing only a few thousand pounds or a large transport aircraft weighing tens of tons, the basic aerodynamic principles are the same.

To understand how airplanes fly, we first need to understand the four forces of flight.

## The Four Forces of Flight

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/4Forces.jpg"
       alt="4Forces"
       style="width: 600px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak" target="_blank">Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge</a>.</em>
</p>

An airplane in flight is affected by four primary forces:

- **Thrust**
- **Drag**
- **Lift**
- **Weight**

**Thrust** is the forward force that moves the airplane through the air. In a propeller aircraft, the engine and propeller produce this force. In a jet aircraft, the engines produce thrust by accelerating air rearward.

**Drag** is the aerodynamic resistance that acts opposite the direction of motion. It includes parasite drag, which comes from the aircraft moving through the air, and induced drag, which is associated with the production of lift.

**Lift** is the upward aerodynamic force generated primarily by the wings.

**Weight** is the downward force caused by gravity acting on the aircraft.

A simple way to think about flight is this: an airplane must generate enough lift to support its weight and enough thrust to overcome drag.

In steady, level, unaccelerated flight, these forces are approximately balanced:

- Lift equals weight.
- Thrust equals drag.

During takeoff, climb, descent, turns, or acceleration, the balance changes. But the basic relationship among these four forces remains central to understanding flight.

The next question is:

**How does the wing generate lift?**

## Lift Is Not Magic

Lift is often explained through two major ideas:

- Newton’s third law of motion
- Bernoulli’s principle

These are sometimes presented as two separate explanations, but it is better to think of them as two different ways of looking at the same physical process.

A wing generates lift because it changes the motion and pressure of the air around it.

## Newton’s Third Law: The Wing Deflects Air Downward

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/Newton.jpg"
       alt="Newton"
       style="width: 400px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Sir_Isaac_Newton,_1689.jpg" target="_blank">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</em>
</p>

Newton’s third law is often summarized as:

> For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

In the case of a wing, the airfoil and angle of attack help turn the airflow downward behind the wing. This downward-moving air is called **downwash**.

If the wing pushes air downward, the air pushes back on the wing in the opposite direction. That upward reaction is part of what we call lift.

This is an important point because there is a common misunderstanding among student pilots.

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/nasa1.gif"
       alt="nasa1"
       style="width: 600px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/wrong2.html" target="_blank">NASA</a>.</em>
</p>

Some people explain lift by saying that air strikes the bottom of the wing and simply pushes it upward. That explanation is incomplete and can be misleading.

Lift is not created only because air hits the bottom of the wing. Instead, the wing shapes and redirects the airflow around it. The production of downwash is a key part of understanding lift from a Newtonian perspective.

## Bernoulli’s Principle: Pressure Differences Around the Wing

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/bernoulli.jpg"
       alt="nasa1"
       style="width: 400px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Portr%C3%A4t_des_Daniel_Bernoulli_-_edit1.jpg" target="_blank">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</em>
</p>

Another way to understand lift is through Bernoulli’s principle.

Daniel Bernoulli was a Swiss mathematician who lived in the 1700s, long before the Wright brothers made their first powered flight in 1903. It is fascinating that principles developed centuries ago can still help explain how modern aircraft fly.

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/venturi.png"
       alt="venturi"
       style="width: 500px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Venturifixed.PNG" target="_blank">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</em>
</p>

Bernoulli’s principle relates the speed of a fluid to its pressure. In simplified terms, when airflow speeds up, its static pressure tends to decrease. When airflow slows down, its static pressure tends to increase.

This helps explain the pressure distribution around a wing.

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/Bernoullis-Principle-Example.jpg"
       alt="Bernoullis-Principle-Example"
       style="width: 500px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.sciencefacts.net/bernoullis-principle-and-equation.html" target="_blank">ScienceFacts</a>.</em>
</p>

As the aircraft moves forward, air flows around the airfoil. Because of the shape of the wing and its angle of attack (also discussed as curvature), the airflow over the upper surface is generally faster and lower in pressure than the airflow beneath the wing. This pressure difference contributes to the upward aerodynamic force.

In other words, the wing creates a pressure pattern around itself. Higher pressure below the wing and lower pressure above the wing help produce lift.

However, this explanation also needs to be handled carefully.

## The Equal Transit Theory Is Wrong

One of the most common misconceptions about lift is the **equal transit theory**.

<p align="center">
  <img src="/images/posts/nasa2.gif"
       alt="nasa2"
       style="width: 600px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <br>
  <em>Source: <a href="https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/VirtualAero/BottleRocket/airplane/wrong1.html" target="_blank">NASA</a>.</em>
</p>

This explanation says that air molecules split at the leading edge of the wing, with some traveling over the top and others traveling underneath. Because the upper surface is curved and appears longer, the air on top supposedly has to travel faster in order to meet the air from the bottom at the trailing edge.

That sounds simple, but it is wrong.

Air that separates at the front of the wing does not have to meet again at the back at the same time. In reality, the air moving over the top of the wing often reaches the trailing edge sooner than the air moving below.

Also, not all airfoils have a dramatically longer upper surface. Some aircraft can even fly inverted, which would be difficult to explain using the equal transit idea alone.

So the statement, “air moves faster over the top because it has a longer distance to travel,” is not a correct explanation of lift.

A better explanation is that the wing’s shape, angle of attack, and interaction with the surrounding airflow create both downwash and pressure differences. Newton’s laws and Bernoulli’s principle are both useful ways to understand that process.

## So Which Explanation Is Correct?

The simple answer is: both Newton and Bernoulli help explain lift, but neither should be reduced to an oversimplified story.

From a Newtonian perspective, the wing generates lift by turning air downward.

From a Bernoulli perspective, the wing generates lift through pressure differences created by the airflow around the airfoil.

These are not competing explanations. They are connected descriptions of the same aerodynamic reality.

More advanced aerodynamic analysis can involve concepts such as circulation, Euler’s equations, and the Navier-Stokes equations. But for most student pilots, understanding Newton’s third law, Bernoulli’s principle, downwash, pressure differences, and the limits of the equal transit theory provides a strong foundation.

## Closing Thoughts

An airplane flies because its wings can generate lift, its engines can produce thrust, and the pilot manages the aircraft within the balance of aerodynamic forces.

The basic idea may sound simple:

> Lift supports weight, and thrust overcomes drag.

But behind that simple statement is a complex and fascinating interaction between air, pressure, motion, wing shape, and angle of attack.

For student pilots, understanding lift is not just about passing a written exam or explaining a textbook diagram. It helps build a more accurate mental model of how the airplane responds in real flight.

And in aviation, better understanding usually leads to better decision-making.
