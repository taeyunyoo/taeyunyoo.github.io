Thunderstorms are one of the weather hazards pilots are trained to avoid.

They can produce severe turbulence, strong updrafts and downdrafts, hail, lightning, heavy precipitation, rapidly changing visibility, and wind shear. For small training aircraft in particular, flying near convective weather can quickly become dangerous.

In this post, I want to discuss why pilots should avoid thunderstorms, using a Piper PA-28 accident as a reminder of how quickly weather-related risk can become fatal.

## A Piper PA-28 Accident in Kentucky

In September 2023, a Piper PA-28 training aircraft crashed near Whitesville, Kentucky.

The aircraft was operated by a flight school and was returning after a night cross-country training flight with an instructor and student on board. During the flight, the aircraft encountered severe turbulence while operating in an area affected by convective weather.

Before discussing the accident further, it is helpful to understand one of the most important weather products involved in convective weather decision-making: the **Convective SIGMET**.

## What Is a Convective SIGMET?

  <img src="/images/posts/convective sigmet.png"
       alt="convective sigmet"
       style="width: 600px; max-width: 100%; height: auto;">

</p>

A **SIGMET** stands for **Significant Meteorological Information**.

It is an aviation weather advisory issued for weather phenomena that may be hazardous to all aircraft. These can include severe turbulence, severe icing, volcanic ash, dust storms, sandstorms, and other significant hazards.

A **Convective SIGMET** is a specific type of SIGMET related to convective weather, especially thunderstorms.

For pilots, a Convective SIGMET is not just another weather note in the briefing. It is an official warning that the atmosphere is producing conditions that can be dangerous to flight.

Convective SIGMETs may be issued for conditions such as:

- lines of thunderstorms,
- embedded thunderstorms,
- large areas of active thunderstorms,
- thunderstorms producing severe turbulence,
- thunderstorms producing hail,
- or thunderstorms associated with tornadoes.

In simple terms, a Convective SIGMET tells pilots that the weather is not just uncomfortable or inconvenient. It may be capable of producing conditions that exceed what an aircraft or pilot can safely manage.

When a pilot sees a Convective SIGMET along the route of flight, the safest decision is often to delay the flight, cancel the flight, or plan a route that provides significant distance from the convective activity.

In the Kentucky Piper PA-28 accident, the aircraft continued into an area of hazardous convective weather. The aircraft encountered extreme conditions, exceeded structural limits, and broke up in flight. Both occupants were fatally injured.

The lesson is direct:

> Thunderstorms are not weather to “push through.” They are weather to avoid.

## Why Thunderstorms Are So Dangerous to Aircraft

Thunderstorms are dangerous because they are not a single hazard. They are a combination of several hazards occurring in the same area, often changing rapidly.

### 1. Severe or Extreme Turbulence

Thunderstorms can produce turbulence strong enough to damage aircraft.

For a small training aircraft, this can be especially dangerous. Severe turbulence can make it difficult or impossible to maintain aircraft control, and extreme turbulence can impose structural loads beyond what the aircraft was designed to withstand.

Even large transport-category aircraft avoid thunderstorms for this reason. The fact that an aircraft is certified, maintained, and properly operated does not mean it is designed to fly through the strongest parts of a thunderstorm.

### 2. Strong Updrafts and Downdrafts

Thunderstorms contain powerful vertical air movement.

Strong updrafts can rapidly lift an aircraft. Strong downdrafts can force an aircraft downward at rates the pilot may not be able to overcome. In a small aircraft, these vertical currents can quickly create loss of control or structural stress.

One particularly dangerous form of downdraft is a **microburst**.

A microburst is a localized, intense downdraft that spreads outward when it reaches the ground. It can produce strong wind shear and rapid changes in airspeed and vertical performance. Close to the ground, especially during takeoff or landing, this can leave the pilot with very little time or altitude to recover.

### 3. Hail and Lightning

Thunderstorms can also produce hail and lightning.

Hail can damage windshields, wings, propellers, antennas, and other aircraft surfaces. Lightning can affect electrical systems and avionics. Although aircraft are designed with lightning protection, that does not mean lightning strikes are harmless or something pilots should intentionally risk.

Another important point is that hail does not always remain directly inside the visible thunderstorm core. Strong storm systems can throw hail significant distances from the main cell.

That is why pilots are taught to give thunderstorms a wide margin.

Avoiding the visible cloud or rain shaft by a small distance is not enough. A safer strategy is to remain well clear of thunderstorm cells, often by at least **20 nautical miles** when possible.

### 4. Loss of Visibility

Thunderstorms can rapidly reduce visibility.

Heavy rain, cloud, lightning, and changing visibility can disorient a pilot, especially at night or in marginal conditions. Even when the aircraft and pilot are legally prepared for instrument flight, unexpected convective weather can create a workload and risk level far beyond a normal IFR flight.

For VFR pilots, entering thunderstorm-related cloud or precipitation can quickly become a loss-of-control scenario.

For IFR pilots, the risk is not eliminated. The aircraft may still be exposed to turbulence, icing, hail, lightning, and severe vertical currents.

Instrument capability does not make thunderstorm penetration safe.

## The Safety Lesson

The lesson from this accident is not complicated, but it is important.

A pilot should never treat thunderstorms as weather to challenge.

Weather decision-making is part of the pilot-in-command’s responsibility. Under preflight action requirements, pilots are expected to become familiar with available information concerning the flight, including weather reports and forecasts, fuel requirements, alternatives, and known traffic delays.

That responsibility does not end when the aircraft takes off.

Pilots must continue monitoring weather conditions, updating their mental picture, and making conservative decisions when conditions change.

In thunderstorm-related decision-making, several questions matter:

- Is there a Convective SIGMET along or near the route?
- Is the storm moving toward the planned flight path?
- Is there a safe route around the weather with sufficient distance?
- Is there enough fuel and daylight to make that route practical?
- Is delaying or canceling the flight the safer decision?
- Am I continuing because it is safe, or because I want the flight to work out?

That last question is often the hardest one.

## Hazardous Attitudes and Weather Decisions

Weather accidents often involve more than weather.

They also involve human judgment.

In the Kentucky accident, one of the most painful details was that the instructor had reportedly made a social media comment before the flight suggesting that they would push through storm activity. Regardless of the intention behind the comment, the flight continued into hazardous weather conditions.

From a safety perspective, this raises concerns about two hazardous attitudes described by the FAA:

- **Macho**: “I can do it.”
- **Invulnerability**: “It will not happen to me.”

These attitudes are dangerous because they make risk feel manageable even when the evidence says otherwise.

A pilot may look at a radar image and think, “We can squeeze through.”  
A pilot may hear a weather warning and think, “It will probably be fine.”  
A pilot may continue because turning back feels inconvenient, embarrassing, or unnecessary.

But aviation does not reward confidence that is not supported by conditions.

The safest pilots are not the ones who try to prove they can handle everything. The safest pilots are often the ones who are willing to say:

> Not today, not through that weather.

## Closing Thoughts

Thunderstorms can look impressive from a distance, but they are unforgiving in flight.

They can produce turbulence, hail, lightning, wind shear, microbursts, and visibility loss. More importantly, they can create a situation where a pilot has fewer options, less time, and less control than expected.

The lesson is simple:

Avoid thunderstorms.

Respect Convective SIGMETs.

Give convective weather more distance than you think you need.

And remember that one of the strongest safety decisions a pilot can make is deciding not to continue.

In aviation, the most dangerous moment is often not when the aircraft is already in trouble.

It is the moment when the pilot convinces themselves, “It will probably be okay.”
